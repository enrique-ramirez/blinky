# Constructions that read as machine-written

A word list catches vocabulary. It does not catch shape, and shape is what gives an assistant away. Prose can pass every register check and still be unmistakable, because the sentences are all the same length, every paragraph opens the same way, and the punctuation has one favourite move.

This file is the second half. The hook checks the items marked *hook*; the rest is judgement, applied by the review agents and by anyone editing prose.

## The dash habit (hook)

An em dash is the assistant's default joint. It glues a clause to an aside, then to a definition, then to a correction, and after four paragraphs the rhythm is the whole voice.

Replace it. A comma for an aside, a colon before a definition or a list, parentheses for something genuinely parenthetical, a full stop when the second half is its own thought. The full stop is usually right, and it is the option people forget.

> The queue is separate: it holds rows that came from the library.

> Detection is a guess, so Settings overrides it.

> Two writers, one record. The second save wins and nobody is told.

En dashes in numeric ranges are fine and are not checked. So is a dialogue dash, which touches the word on one side only.

## Negative parallelism (hook)

"It is not just a cache, it is a contract." "Not only faster, but simpler." The construction promises a reversal and delivers a synonym. It is the single most recognisable sentence in machine prose.

State the second half and drop the first. If the contrast is real, make it a comparison with a named alternative.

## The tricolon

Three items, rising, with the third the longest. Once in a document is rhetoric. Three times is a tic, and a reader stops hearing the content.

Count the lists in anything you write. When several land on three, one of them wants to be two, and another wants to be four or a sentence.

## Copula avoidance (hook)

"Serves as", "stands as", "acts as", "boasts", "features". These exist to avoid writing "is" and "has", which are the right words. Nobody says a function serves as a parser.

## Participle chains

"Handling the retry, ensuring the connection stays open, allowing the caller to continue." Three `-ing` clauses hanging off one verb, none of them committing to a subject. Break them into sentences with subjects. It costs a few words and reads at half the effort.

## False ranges

"From configuration to deployment." "From naming to architecture." The form implies a scale, and the two ends are usually just two examples. Name the two things, or name the category.

## Elegant variation

Calling the same thing a queue, then a buffer, then a holding area, so a paragraph does not repeat itself. Repetition is not a defect in technical prose. A reader tracking three names for one object has to hold a glossary, and will eventually guess wrong about whether the third name is a fourth object.

Pick the name the code uses and use it every time.

## Uniform rhythm

Machine prose runs fifteen to twenty-five words a sentence, forever. Human prose does not. A four-word sentence after a long one is the cheapest way to sound like somebody talking, and it is the thing that survives translation into any voice.

Read a paragraph aloud. If you never run out of breath and never stop early, it is flat.

## The epigram opener

Every paragraph starting with a short bolded pronouncement, then three sentences of support. It reads well once. Down a whole document it becomes a liturgy, and the reader starts skimming the bold and ignoring the argument, which inverts what the emphasis was for.

Vary the entry. Some paragraphs open with the example, some with the exception, some with a question the reader is already asking. Keep the bolded epigram for the two or three claims that carry the document.

## Mechanical emphasis

Bold applied to the first clause of every bullet, or to any noun that felt important. Emphasis works by contrast, so a page with forty bold spans has none. Ration it.

## Formatting tells (hook)

Curly quotes, emoji in technical prose, and Title Case On Headings. Straight quotes, no emoji, sentence case.

## Stacked hedges (hook)

"Could potentially", "may possibly", "might conceivably". One hedge is honest. Two is padding. Where the uncertainty is real, name it: say which part you could not check, and why.

## The restating close

A final paragraph that summarises what the reader just read. It exists because the writer wanted an ending, not because the reader needed one. Stop at the last real point.

## What to do with a flagged line

Rewrite the sentence, do not swap the word. Substituting "utilise" for "leverage" leaves the shape intact, and the shape was the problem. Say the thing the way you would say it out loud, then check that it still means what the code does.
